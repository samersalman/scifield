# Results

We evaluated the scientific literature of orthopedic and general surgery along four axes of a single integrated framework, asking in turn whether a field's *evidence quality* leads or lags its *research volume* (F1), whether *semantic* and *structural* novelty form two genuinely distinct dimensions of innovation and how they relate to citation impact (F2), and whether the *citation-graph topology* of a research area improves forecasts of which topics will *emerge* over the next three years (F3). The framework yields one positive structural finding and two rigorously characterized nulls. The dual-novelty construct (F2) holds: semantic and structural novelty are near-independent axes, and the most novel-and-disruptive papers carry the *lowest* age-fair citation impact — a small but highly reliable, counterintuitive gradient. The two questions about a topic's *temporal evolution* return clean nulls under their pre-registered tests: there is no detectable population-level lead/lag cascade between evidence quality and volume (F1), and adding the citation graph does not improve — and modestly worsens — three-year emergence forecasting over a graph-free baseline (F3). Both nulls were specified before the analyses were run (F1 internally pre-registered; F3 OSF-registered), so they are credible negative results rather than failed searches. The contribution is therefore the validated multi-axis pipeline together with the F2 finding and the two honest nulls that sharpen what the framework can and cannot detect.

Throughout, we report the exact statistics from each analysis and tie every claim to its figure. Where a null is the result, we report it as a null.

## F1 — The epistemic cascade: does evidence quality lead or lag research volume? (pre-registered; NULL)

**Pre-registration.** Before any F1 computation, we committed a pre-registration of this analysis (`docs/preregistrations/PR3_epistemic_cascade.md`, committed `bab917d`, ahead of the F1 compute commit `015ee9b`; the ordering is git-timestamped and internal to the project). The hypothesis, the qualifying-topic filter, the primary quality metric, the directional test, and the multiple-comparison correction were all fixed in advance. The analysis was run once. We emphasize the pre-registration up front because it is what makes the negative result below a credible finding rather than a discarded hypothesis.

**Question.** Within a research topic, does the *quality* (maturity) of its evidence base *lead* or *lag* the growth of its research *volume*? Intuitively, a field might first accumulate volume and only later mature toward stronger study designs (volume leading quality), or stronger early evidence might pull volume in behind it (quality leading volume). F1 tests, at the population level across many topics, whether either directional pattern exists.

**Setup.** We joined the extracted evidence records to the semantic-novelty table over leaf-level topics — 69,339 papers across 149 leaf topics spanning 1995–2026 — and restricted to the **138 qualifying topics** with at least 30 papers and at least 8 years of coverage at 5 or more papers per year. The primary measure of quality is the *mean evidence tier* (RCT = 4, cohort = 3, case-control = 2, case-series = 1; reviews and other designs excluded, yielding 65.4% coverage); the robustness measure is the *RCT share*. For each topic we computed a cross-correlation function (lags ±5 years) and a Granger test (fixed lag 3, on differenced series) in both directions, then pooled across topics with a Benjamini-Hochberg FDR correction at q < 0.05 and a pooled fixed-effects panel-Granger test.

**Result — a clean null.** Of the 138 qualifying topics, **0** show an FDR-significant directional Granger relationship in either direction (number directional = 0; fraction directional = **0.000**, far below the pre-registered 0.20 bar; dominant direction = "none"). The pooled panel test is non-significant both ways (quality→volume p = 0.277; volume→quality p = 0.739). The conclusion is unchanged under the robustness measure: the RCT-share panel is also non-significant in both directions (p = 0.950 and p = 0.956), so the primary and robustness analyses agree, both null. (The 138-topic denominator conservatively retains 10 topics whose quality series was not evaluable, which can only make the fraction harder to clear, not easier.)

**Why this is a true null, not an underpowered miss.** Two diagnostics show the negative result reflects the absence of a population-level signal rather than a lack of sensitivity. First, the raw, uncorrected tests are not empty — 15 topics reach raw p < 0.05 — but the multiple-comparison correction over m = 252 hypotheses requires the smallest p-value to be ≤ 1.98 × 10⁻⁴ to survive, whereas the actual minimum observed is 7.75 × 10⁻⁴; nothing clears the corrected threshold. The handful of nominally significant topics is what one expects by chance at this number of tests. Second, the cross-correlation peak-lag distribution is broad and roughly symmetric around a median of 0 years (mean ≈ −0.1 yr), with no concentrated lead or lag — there is no displaced peak that a directional cascade would produce. In this corpus, then, evidence quality and research volume rise together without either systematically leading the other at the population level.

## F2 — Dual novelty: two distinct innovation axes and their citation impact (Gate G3 PROCEED; HOLDS)

This is the axis that holds, and we give it corresponding weight while keeping its effect sizes in proportion.

**Two near-independent axes.** *Semantic* novelty measures how unlike its predecessors a paper's content is; *structural* novelty (the CD index, a disruption measure) captures whether a paper's citing literature breaks from or consolidates the prior work it builds on. If these two collapse into one axis, the dual-novelty construct is redundant. They do not: across the four metric pairings, the largest absolute correlation is Pearson r = **0.1828** / Spearman ρ = **0.1751** (both below the 0.4 independence bar; N = 81,733 complete cases). Semantic and structural novelty are weakly correlated, distinct dimensions, and Gate G3 was signed PROCEED on this basis (`docs/gates/G3_dual_novelty.md`).

**A balanced 2×2.** Splitting both axes at their medians defines four archetypes, all well-populated with no degenerate quadrant: *disruptive-novel* (high-high) 22,662 papers (0.277), *novel-consolidating* (high-low) 18,210 (0.223), *conventional-disruptive* (low-high) 19,474 (0.238), and *incremental* (low-low) 21,392 (0.262). The framework partitions the corpus cleanly rather than piling papers into one corner.

**New enrichment — the counterintuitive impact inversion.** The V1-S15 enrichment links these archetypes to citation *impact*, measured as each paper's within-year citation percentile (an age-fair ranking that compares a paper only against others from its own year, so older papers are not credited simply for having had longer to accrue citations). A Kruskal-Wallis test across the four archetypes is highly significant — **H = 1938.58, p < 1e-300** — on n = 81,738 labeled papers. The associated effect size is **ε² = 0.024**, meaning the archetype explains roughly 2.4% of the rank variance: a *small* but, given the sample and p-value, *highly reliable* gradient. The direction is the notable part. Median impact percentile rises monotonically across the archetypes in a way that *inverts* the naive expectation that the most novel and disruptive work is the most impactful: disruptive-novel **0.433** < novel-consolidating **0.502** < conventional-disruptive **0.523** < incremental **0.607**. The most novel-and-disruptive papers sit *lowest* on age-fair citation impact, and the incremental papers sit highest. This reconfirms and now quantifies the inversion flagged as the "surprising-to-expert" finding at Gate G3. We stress that the effect is modest in magnitude (a few percent of variance); the strength of the result is the consistency and statistical reliability of the *ordering*, not the size of the gap.

**Temporal drift.** The archetype mix shifts over time. From the pre-2000 cohort to the 2020-and-later cohort, the conventional-disruptive share rises by **+0.083** and the novel-consolidating share falls by **−0.055** (matching the G3 value of −0.056). This drift should not be over-read at its left edge: structural-novelty coverage is thinner in the earliest years (~0.95 pre-2000 falling toward ~0.90 by ≥2020), so the early-cohort shares rest on a slightly smaller denominator.

## F3 — Graph forecasting: does citation-graph structure improve emergence forecasts? (Gate G4 NULL; NULL)

**Question and framing.** F3 asks whether the *citation-graph topology* of a research area helps forecast which topics will *emerge* (sharply grow) over the following three years, beyond what is already captured by each topic's temporal and feature dynamics. We compare a heterogeneous-graph neural network (HGT), which passes information along the citation graph, against an otherwise-matched *graph-free* baseline (`no_graph`) that uses the same node features without graph message-passing — the apples-to-apples "what does adding the graph buy you" contrast pre-registered in PR2 (OSF DOI 10.17605/OSF.IO/XP94F). The evaluation is on a *sealed* test set fixed in advance (forecast-origin years 2021–2022; n_train = 1298, n_test = 138, with 13 positives, ≈ 9.4%), with no retraining or label/horizon re-tuning after unsealing.

**Result — the temporal dynamics carry the signal; the graph does not.** On the sealed test, the graph-free baseline is the strongest of five models at emergence AUC = **0.8043**, ahead of HGT at **0.7809** (the others: naive 0.4074, ARIMA 0.6529, MLP 0.7009). The pre-registered ablation — HGT minus no_graph — is **−2.34pp**, against a required **+5pp** bar: not merely short of the threshold but on the *wrong side of zero*. The graph model would have needed to reach ≈ 0.8543 AUC to clear the bar; instead it trails the graph-free model. So the temporal and feature dynamics of a topic *alone* (no_graph, AUC 0.80) carry the three-year emergence signal, and the citation-graph topology adds nothing on top of them.

**The graph model is also worse-calibrated.** A paired per-topic Wilcoxon test on Brier loss (lower = better) is highly significant at **p = 2.63 × 10⁻¹¹**, but the direction *favors the baseline*: HGT's per-topic forecasts carry systematically higher error. Mechanistically, HGT over-predicts — it places forecast mass in the 0.4–0.8 probability range for many topics whose realized emergence is essentially zero, and it has the worst share-forecast error of the five models (share-MAPE 0.5729) — whereas the graph-free baseline keeps its predictions near the ≈9.4% base rate and stays roughly calibrated. The significance here therefore *reinforces* the null rather than rescuing the graph model: the citation graph does not help forecasting and degrades calibration.

**Honest context on the small test set.** The test set is small and high-variance (n = 138, 13 positives), and the validation-to-test ranking did shift on it (no_graph 0.701→0.804, HGT 0.737→0.781). We flag this as honest context, but it does not rescue F3: even granting the variance, HGT lands on the wrong side of the bar, not near it, and the result is robust to alternate emergence-label definitions (HGT is no better — in fact lower — under every variant). Gate G4 records this as a signed NULL FINDING (`docs/gates/G4_forecasting.md`): on the sealed test, citation-graph structure adds no forecasting value beyond the temporal/feature node dynamics.

**F1 and F3 together.** F1 and F3 are two independent, pre-specified probes of how a topic *evolves over time* — whether evidence quality leads volume, and whether graph structure forecasts emergence — and both return honest nulls. Read together, they say that the temporal trajectory of a topic in this corpus is not predictable from the population-level quality/volume coupling or from the citation graph's topology; the finding that survives is the *cross-sectional* structure of innovation in F2.

## Bonus — cross-journal, specialty, and institutional seeding (EXPLORATORY; non-gating)

The following observations are **exploratory and do not enter the finding count** that determines the paper's scope; they characterize *who* tends to publish on a topic first and are offered as descriptive context only, not as tested hypotheses. A metadata guard passed before running them: 113,413 / 121,908 papers (~93%) carry an affiliation, so the institutional panel runs, and country information is present on 16,258 / 29,159 institutions (~56%), so the geographic panel runs with the coverage caveat stated below.

**Cross-journal seeding.** Scoring each topic by which journals publish in it earliest (on `journal_slug`, so the *Archives → JAMA Surgery* rename collapses to a single node), the leading *seed* journals are *Br J Surg* (0.74), *J Bone Joint Surg / JBJS* (0.70), and *Clin Orthop / CORR* (0.69), while *Arthroscopy* (0.46), *J Arthroplasty* (0.48), and *Spine* (0.54) tend to *follow*. Figure: `docs/figures/bonus_seeding_network.png`.

**Specialty contrast.** Orthopedic journals seed topics slightly earlier than general-surgery journals (0.74 vs 0.68). General surgery skews more disruptive-novel (0.26 vs 0.15) and orthopedics skews more incremental (0.38 vs 0.29). Because the F1 cascade is null overall, any per-specialty cascade reading is exploratory only. Figure: `docs/figures/bonus_specialty_contrast.png`.

**Institutional and geographic seeding.** The top seeding institutions are Mayo Clinic (0.82), Hospital for Special Surgery (0.78), UCSF (0.78), Harvard (0.77), and Johns Hopkins (0.76); the top seeding countries are the US (0.99) > GB (0.88) > JP (0.80) > CA (0.79) > DE (0.76). Figure: `docs/figures/bonus_geographic.png`.

**Coverage caveat (verbatim).** "Geographic seeding is exploratory: a country_code is present on only 56% of institutions in the corpus institutions table (16,258/29,159); blank-country rows are dropped (never imputed). Country ranks are over the affiliated, country-tagged subset only."
